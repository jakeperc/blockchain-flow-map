## What happened

On 17 June 2016 the Ethereum Foundation warned that an attacker was draining The DAO, a contract holding ether for its token holders, into a child DAO [1]. It described a recursive calling flaw: the attacker's request to split from The DAO called itself inside the split, collecting ether many times over in a single transaction [1].

The Foundation noted that the child DAO's own rules kept the attacker from withdrawing for at least another 27 days, which left time to act [1].

## Why it matters

The response is what made it a landmark. Rather than reverse any transaction, the network adopted new rules at a fork block that moved the ether out of the child DAO into a recovery contract for token holders [3].

On chain that shows as something no transaction can do: the child DAO held {{b1.at2}} at block {{b1.block2}} and {{b1.at3}} one block later, moved by what the Foundation called an irregular state change [3]. Not everyone accepted it; the same post tells users who wanted to keep following the chain without the fork how to do so [3].

## What happened next

The Foundation reported a smooth fork, with roughly 85% of miners on the new chain, and the recovery contract already returning ether to token holders [3].
